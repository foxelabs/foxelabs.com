Every day at 5 PM India time, a new story goes out on
[Elsewhere, Once](/media/channels/elsewhere-once/): a short, true, fact-checked
story from somewhere in the world, about a minute long. It lands on YouTube,
Instagram, Facebook and X at the same time, and half an hour later it has its
own page on [elsewhereonce.com](https://elsewhereonce.com) with the written
version, the sources and the fact-check notes.

Nobody on our side touches any of it. There is no editor, no voice actor, no
animator and no one with a phone open at 5 PM. Picking the story, checking the
facts, writing the script, recording the voice, animating the film, posting it
and publishing the web page are all done by Claude, on a schedule.

We're a software lab, so we built the channel the way we build software: small
tools, written instructions under version control, checks that fail loudly, and
a deploy that runs itself. This post is how it works, and what we learned
putting it together.

## The short answer

**Elsewhere, Once runs on three scheduled Claude Code routines.** Each one is a
Claude agent that starts at a fixed time in a cloud environment, checks out the
channel's repository and follows a Markdown instruction file in it.

| Time (IST) | Routine | What it leaves behind |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5:36 AM | Story, voice and film | A research doc, a voiceover with word timings, an animated film, the finished MP4 and its cover |
| 5:00 PM | Posting | The video live on YouTube, Instagram, Facebook and X, with each network's own title and caption |
| 5:30 PM | Website | A new story page on elsewhereonce.com, built, committed and deployed |

The first video went out on 19 November 2026. Since then the channel has
posted every day without a manual step.

## Why automate a channel at all

A daily channel is a deployment problem wearing a creative hat. The hard part is
not making one good video; it's making one every single day, at the same
quality, with the facts right, for months. A team of one can't hold that pace by
hand next to other work, and the first thing to slip is always the boring part:
checking sources.

So we split the job the way we'd split a release pipeline. Each stage has an
input, an output and a written definition of done. If a stage can't meet its
definition, it stops and says why instead of shipping something half-right.

## Instructions are files in the repo

The routines' prompts are almost empty. Each one says, in effect, "check out the
repository and follow this file". The real instructions live next to the code:

- `tools/daily-film.md` is the morning routine: research, script, voice, film
  and covers.
- `PUBLISHING.md` is the website routine: how to turn a new video into a story
  page.
- The posting routine has its own checklist in the same style.

That one decision made everything else easier. Changing how the channel works
means editing Markdown and committing it, so every change has a diff, a message
and a date. When a run goes wrong, we fix the instruction that allowed it, not
the run. And the files double as documentation: there is no other description
of the process to drift out of date.

The instructions are written as checklists with exact formats rather than as
advice. "Title it exactly `Elsewhere, Once — YYYY-MM-DD — <story>`" works;
"give it a clear title" doesn't, because the next routine has to find that doc
by its title.

## Step 1: picking a story people will watch to the end

A short lives or dies in its first two seconds, so a worthy story is not
enough. The morning routine brainstorms at least eight candidates from different
regions and fields, and scores each from 1 to 5 on hook, surprise, payoff,
visual potential and global appeal. If nothing scores at least 4 on both hook
and surprise, it brainstorms again.

The winner then has to pass hard rules: real and documented, at least two
independent reliable sources, not breaking news, not a tragedy told for shock,
not about a private individual, and not partisan. To avoid repeats, the routine
first lists every earlier story doc and rotates continents and fields.

That's how the channel ended up with stories like the
[city that fought traffic with mimes](https://elsewhereonce.com/stories/bogota-traffic-mimes/)
and the [slime that designed a railway](https://elsewhereonce.com/stories/tokyo-slime-mould-railway/):
odd enough to stop a thumb, solid enough to source.

## Step 2: fact-check before a word is written

Claude searches the web and reads the actual source pages, then checks every
date, number, name and outcome it plans to use against them. Where sources
disagree, or an outcome had several causes, the script has to say so. If a
story can't be verified well, it's dropped and the next candidate takes its
place.

Only then does the routine write anything, and it writes exactly **one** research
doc per run, in Claude Docs. The doc holds everything the later stages need:
the summary, the voiceover script, a scene plan, captions, the YouTube title and
description, the Instagram and Facebook caption, the X post and its first reply,
the sources, and the fact-check notes. Every later stage reads from this doc, so
the video, the posts and the web page can't tell different versions of the
story.

## Step 3: a script in simple English

Many of the channel's viewers know only a little English. The instructions
spell out what that means in practice: everyday words ("use", not "utilise"),
one idea per sentence and none over 18 words, no idioms or slang, active voice,
and big numbers rounded when the exact figure doesn't matter. Before saving,
Claude rereads the script as someone who learned English at school a long time
ago, and replaces anything they might not follow.

Every script opens with the channel's signature, "Elsewhere, once…", and
follows the same shape: hook, problem, twist, outcome, one-line takeaway. We
started with a hard 60-second cap and dropped it. Cutting a sentence that makes
the twist land, just to hit a number, made worse videos. Now length follows the
story: most run 50 to 75 seconds, and nothing goes past 90.

## Step 4: a voice, with every word timed

The voiceover comes from the ElevenLabs API through a small, dependency-free
Node script in the repository, `tools/voiceover.mjs`. It sends the script with
the channel's voice and settings to the `with-timestamps` endpoint, which
returns the audio **and** the start and end time of every character. The script
turns that into a JSON file with the timing of every beat, sentence and word.

Two details matter more than they look:

- **Pause tightening.** ElevenLabs leaves generous pauses between paragraphs.
  The script trims each long pause with ffmpeg and shifts every timestamp to
  match, so the audio and the timings stay in sync.
- **The key never touches the agent.** In the cloud environment the ElevenLabs
  key is a network secret: the environment's proxy adds it to requests for
  `api.elevenlabs.io` on the way out. The script sends no key, and Claude can't
  read, print or store it. (One gotcha: Node's `fetch` ignores `HTTPS_PROXY`
  unless `NODE_USE_ENV_PROXY` is set when Node starts, so the script restarts
  itself with it set.)

Each take costs credits, so the instructions only allow real takes, with at most
two retries if the voice runs too long.

## Step 5: an animated film, timed to the words

The films are colourful, flat storybook animation, made as a Motion artifact in
Claude: a film is a set of HTML, CSS and JavaScript modules, one per scene, plus
a soft score. The house style is fixed in the instructions (a 1080 × 1920
frame, 60 fps, the channel's palette and fonts), so the routine never has to ask
anyone a question.

The voice's word timings drive the edit. Every scene cut sits in a pause, never
mid-sentence. Every caption appears on its first word and leaves just after its
last. The film opens on a drawn map that zooms into the place, and ends with
the channel's postmark stamping down.

Then the routine checks its own work before anything is rendered: it runs each
module through `node --check`, renders a still from the middle of every scene
and looks at them side by side on a contact sheet, fixing any scene that is a
plain name card, a caption that overlaps, or anything cut off at the edges.

Finally it renders the real video: every frame at 60 fps through a headless
browser into ffmpeg, the score to a WAV, then the voice and score mixed to about
−14 LUFS and muxed into an MP4. The whole film step takes about twelve minutes.
The one thing to watch here is fonts. The offline renderer doesn't fetch web
fonts, so without help the captions fall back to a different typeface. The
render ships the film's font files with it, and the routine checks a still for
the right face before it renders the video.

## Step 6: covers from the film itself

Our first covers were drawn from scratch, and they looked artificial next to
the video. So now the cover is a real still from the film: the routine renders a
frame from the scene that sells the story best (the place and its characters,
bright and busy), puts the title on a card in the film's own colours and fonts,
and checks that no burned-in caption peeks out around it. The thumbnail looks
exactly like the video because it is the video.

## Step 7: posting to four networks

At 5 PM the posting routine picks up the finished MP4 and its cover, and posts
them to YouTube, Instagram, Facebook and X through each network's API, with the
title, description and caption the research doc already holds for that network.

Most of the work here was done earlier, in the doc. Each network gets its own
copy, written to its own rules: a YouTube description that ends with the
sources, an Instagram and Facebook caption with a handful of hashtags, and an X
post that stands on its own without sound, counted to 280 characters by code
rather than by eye. Links go in a first reply on X rather than in the post,
because X tends to show posts with links to fewer people.

## Step 8: the website writes itself

Half an hour later, the website routine reads the channel's YouTube feed. A
video is new if its ID isn't already in one of the story files in the repo,
which makes the run safe to repeat: run it twice and the second run finds
nothing to do.

For each new video it finds the matching research doc, writes a story file (the
written version, 300 to 400 words, plus every source and the fact-check notes),
runs the build, commits it and pushes to `main`. Cloudflare Workers Builds
deploys on push. Each story also gets a pin on the home page's world map and a
stamp in its album.

`PUBLISHING.md` opens with a sentence we wrote on purpose: *nobody reviews the
result before it goes live*. So the safety net is the build. Every field is
typed, the build fails if one is missing or wrong, and the routine only pushes a
story that builds. If a video has no clearly matching doc, it isn't published,
and the report says why.

## What made it reliable

Most of what we learned is ordinary engineering, applied to an agent:

- **Write checklists, not briefs.** Exact titles, field rules, word limits and
  file names. Anything the next stage relies on gets a fixed format.
- **Keep one source of truth.** The research doc feeds the film, the posts and
  the web page, so nothing can drift.
- **Make runs safe to repeat.** "New" means "not already in the repo", and each
  run creates exactly one doc.
- **Let the agent check its own output.** Syntax checks, contact sheets of
  stills, frames pulled from the final MP4, a build before every push.
- **Fail loudly and narrowly.** A stage that can't finish keeps what it has,
  writes down the blocker and lets the other stages carry on.
- **Keep secrets out of the agent's reach.** Network secrets mean there is no
  key in a prompt, a file or a log.
- **Cap the spend.** Paid steps, like voice takes, have a retry limit written
  into the instructions.

## What's next

The same three routines don't know anything about this particular channel
except what's in their instruction files. That means a second channel is mostly
a new set of files: a new voice, a new look, a new kind of story. We're working
out which one comes next.

In the meantime, there's a new true story every day at
[elsewhereonce.com](https://elsewhereonce.com) and on
[@elsewhereonce](https://www.youtube.com/@elsewhereonce). Made by a pipeline,
checked like a newsroom.
